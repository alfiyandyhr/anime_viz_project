ERA 01 1963–1979

A new ritual.
A story every week.

Japanese animation existed before television. In 1963, a robot boy helped bring it into the weekly rhythm of everyday life.

Osamu Tezuka’s Astro Boy began its Japanese television run on January 1, 1963. Its half-hour serial format became an important model for television anime. The series also reached overseas audiences during the 1960s, including a US broadcast in 1963.

1963
Astro Boy arrives on television

A milestone in the development of modern TV anime, rather than the beginning of Japanese animation itself.

ERA 02 1980–1989

The screen
gets bigger.

Television was only one home for anime. The 1980s also left a distinctive legacy of theatrical animation.

Studio Ghibli began in 1985, after the release of Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind in 1984. Its official chronology places Castle in the Sky in 1986, followed by My Neighbor Totoro and Grave of the Fireflies in 1988.

1985
A new studio takes shape

Ghibli’s filmography would make room for fantasy, childhood, and the memory of war.

Historical source: Studio Ghibli chronology ↗

ERA 03 1990–1999

Stories cross
more borders.

Anime’s international circulation did not begin in the 1990s. But this decade supplied another generation of shared reference points.

One documented example is Pokémon: the franchise’s official history records the animated series’ North American premiere in 1998. It joined a longer history of Japanese animation being translated and distributed beyond Japan.

1998
Pokémon reaches North American TV

A concrete moment in international distribution, not evidence that every anime travelled in the same way.

Historical source: The Pokémon Company ↗

ERA 04 2000–2009

New tools.
The same imagination.

Digital production was a transition across years and studios, not a switch flipped at the start of a decade.

Toei Animation’s company history dates the beginning of its TV production digitization using RETAS software to 1997. That change provides context for the 2000s: drawing, colouring, and compositing could take different routes to the finished frame. “Digital” does not necessarily mean 3D animation.

2003
Spirited Away wins an Academy Award

The 2001 film won Animated Feature at the 2003 Oscars, another point of international recognition.

These title, genre, and studio records do not capture production pipelines; the charts cannot measure digitization directly.

ERA 05 2010–2019

A season in Japan.
A conversation elsewhere.

Online distribution opened another route between a Japanese release and an overseas audience.

Crunchyroll announced streaming for Attack on Titan in April 2013, a concrete example of a seasonal series reaching viewers through a licensed online service. Availability still depended on region and rights; streaming did not remove every border.

2013
A seasonal title reaches streaming viewers

Attack on Titan’s licensing is one example of the decade’s distribution landscape.

Historical source: Crunchyroll’s 2013 announcement ↗

The next era

The Association of Japanese Animations publishes industry reports that examine markets and distribution, including overseas activity. That wider economic picture is different from this archive’s view of current AniList list users.

About / Data

Selection. AniList’s GraphQL API returns up to 10 non-adult anime with Japan as country of origin for each first-release year, sorted by current popularity. Unreleased entries are excluded. Seasons, films, specials, OVA, and ONA entries are distinct records; franchises are not collapsed.

What the numbers mean. Popularity counts current AniList user lists, including planning and completed lists. Scores are current AniList averages, not contemporaneous reviews. Genre percentages divide appearances by selected titles in the period; multiple genres per title mean they need not sum to 100%. Main-studio appearances count a title once for each credited main studio, so co-productions can overlap.

Gaps. Some early years contain fewer than 10 eligible records. A confirmed empty year is distinct from an unavailable query. Missing scores, artwork, genres, and studio credits are shown as unknown. The current year is provisional. Cached data’s retrieval date and any unavailable or stale years are displayed beside the archive.

Historical context. The linked sources alongside each era document specific milestones. Era names are editorial framing; AniList data does not establish historical audience sizes, market shares, or causes of change.
